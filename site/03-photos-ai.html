Chapter 3 · AI in the Photos App

Time: about 15 minutes · Difficulty: easy

Your photos already live in the Photos app — the one that opens when you tap a picture file. What a lot of people don't know is that the Edit button hides one of the friendliest set of AI tools Microsoft ships: remove a whole background, erase a photobomber, restyle a picture, or clean up an old scanned snapshot — all in a couple of clicks, all right-click-and-undo-able.

This chapter is the desktop / laptop side of things. (Our AI on Your Phone guide covers doing the same magic from your phone's gallery.)

3.1 · Open the app the boring-but-reliable way

Step 1. Click Start and type Photos, then press Enter. (Or double-click any photo in File Explorer — Explorer opens it in Photos by default.)

Step 2. Click any photo in your library (thumbnails scroll down the middle of the window), then click the pencil-ish icon that says "Edit" — top center of the photo view, or right-click the image and pick Edit.

What you'll see: the photo moves center-screen and a toolbar appears along the top or the left edge with labels like Crop, Adjustments, Filters, Background, Restyle (older versions: a row of buttons along the bottom, same names). Every tool described below exists somewhere in that Edit view — just look for the matching name.

Good to know: If Windows offers to open your photos with something else, that was a one-time dialog from somebody double-clicking — go Start > Photos > right-click the app tile > Pin to taskbar once, and this stops being an issue forever.

3.2 · Remove the background (the wow-effect tool)

This is the one that makes people gasp: your photo, with the background gone in one click, saved as a new image with transparency ready for a card, slide, or Christmas letter.

Step 1. With your photo open in Edit view, click Background in the toolbar.

Step 2. A button appears that says Remove background. Click it. A few seconds… done: your subject stays, most of the rest of the picture becomes a grey checkered pattern (that's the universal symbol for "now transparent").

Step 3. Refine if the cutout is imperfect: two small tools appear — "Add" (brush over areas that should have stayed) and "Remove" (brush over areas that should have gone). Drag the mouse over the picture to paint; release, and it re-cuts. Also useful: a small brush size slider for delicate work.

Step 4. Click Apply or Next (bottom-right), then Save or "Save a copy" in the corner menu. Always save a copy, not overwrite, as with everything in this book — original photos are sacred.

What you'll see: Windows prompts nothing else; the new file lands in your Pictures folder (or the same folder the original came from), usually named something like "IMG_1001 - Copy". If you don't see the checkerboard pattern, look for a "Download quality" or format row when saving: choose PNG, which is the format that keeps transparency.

Good to know: On most PCs, background removal is fully free and runs on the device. On some feature sets Microsoft marks it as a Premium item (a small badge appears) that wants a Microsoft 365 subscription for higher-resolution saving — but the low-resolution version stays free on almost every machine. If you get a "sign in" prompt, sign in with your Microsoft account and the free version continues without a purchase.

3.3 · Erase a photobomber ("Remove objects")

Step 1. In Edit view, find the row of tools and click Adjustments or scroll the toolbar until you see a tool named "Remove objects", Erase, or "Magic Eraser" (names vary by year; it's always an eraser-ish icon).

Step 2. A brush appears with a size slider. Drag across the unwanted object — the stranger in the background, a power line, your ex (we don't judge).

Step 3. Let go, wait a breath, and the app politely fills the hole using the scene around it. Wrong result? Undo (top-left, the curved arrow, or Ctrl + Z) and brush differently — smaller, tighter strokes give better results than one big swoop.

What you'll see: a light blue wash over the area you brushed, then the area smoothly redraws — like watching a sky repair its own clouds. There are no progress bars: small images finish in a few seconds.

Honest note: this is AI inpainting, and it does not always win. Complex backgrounds (sidewalks with texture, water, crowd scenes) can leave smudges. When it fails, crop wider instead. Chapter 8's troubleshooting includes the "smudge" fix.

3.4 · Restyle Image (Copilot+ PCs and newer Photos versions)

Some versions of Photos include a Restyle tool with dozens of art styles — watercolor, comic, oil pastel, that kind of thing — that repaint your photo in the style you pick. Where it's available:

Step 1. In Edit view, click Restyle (a sparkle or palette icon).

Step 2. A short horizontal row of style thumbnails scrolls under your photo. Click one to see it preview on the photo.

Step 3. Adjust the intensity slider if offered (how heavily the style applies), then Apply, then Save a copy as before.

What you'll see: the photo re-renders in 2–10 seconds; several styles queue instantly, a couple take noticeably longer the first time (they download a small model, which is a one-time few-megabytes affair over your internet).

Good to know: Restyle needs a modern machine (a Neural Processing Unit, NPU — the "Copilot+ PC" class, or a decent graphics card) and the current Photos version from the Microsoft Store. If you see it, use it; if you don't, background and erase still work on most PCs from the last several years.

3.5 · One-tap cleanups that deserve a shout

Two tools that don't say "AI" but are the same tech underneath, and take ten seconds:

  • Enhance (wand icon in Adjustments): one click auto-fixes light, contrast, and color. Two clicks if you use it twice on the same photo — the second click usually overshoots, as with everything with the word "auto."
  • Increase resolution / "Upscale" (in Adjustments, modern versions): doubles a small photo's size with AI smoothing. Genuine life-saver for old screenshots and tiny saved-from-web pictures you want in a family newsletter.

3.6 · Copy text out of a picture (that's Snipping)

This is the feature people mean when they say "photos had an AI thing that read the text in the image." Text copying lives in the Snipping Tool, not Photos:

Step 1. Press Windows key + Shift + S (or Windows key + PrtSc on some setups), and drag a rectangle over whatever is on your screen that contains text.

Step 2. A small notification pops up in the lower right saying snip saved. Click it (or open Start > type Snipping Tool > Enter, and open the latest snip).

Step 3. With the snip open, click the Text actions button in the toolbar — the tool highlights every bit of text it finds, and offers Copy all text (and a redact button that black-boxes anything you picked). Click Copy all text, then paste anywhere with Ctrl + V.

What you'll see: a subtle yellow-ish highlight appears over detected text lines while the Text actions panel is open. One click and it's in your clipboard — great for receipts, code from tutorial videos, and phone numbers on screenshots.

That's the whole Photos / snips feature tour. It is genuinely more approachable than online tools because they run on your PC, save to your PC, and don't dump your vacation photos to a third-party "free upload" website. It's the kind of tool that's safe to hand your aunt at Thanksgiving — and in this guide's humble opinion, that's the highest bar Microsoft's photo tools ever get to clear.
